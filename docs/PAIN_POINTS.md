# Build the checker that would have stopped billions

The people who "move big money" are mostly not traders. They are the treasury, liquidity, margin, risk, product-control, operations and compliance staff whose job is to say *yes, no, or wait* before cash leaves the building. The biggest documented losses came from these checks failing, not from one bad bet: a margin increase that was never approved at Credit Suisse before Archegos cost it about **$5.5bn**, a $1.6bn pile of unexplained profit that UBS controllers never challenged, a risk model with errors that hid over **$6bn** of losses at JPMorgan, 97 warning emails ignored at Knight Capital before it lost **$460m in 45 minutes**, and a "six-eyes" approval that let Citi wire **$900m** instead of $7.8m. Day-to-day, the pain is quieter but measurable: about **45% of margin calls are disputed**, only **17–22% of treasurers can see their cash in real time**, and surveillance teams drown in false alarms. Existing software handles the routine, rule-based layer well. The unsolved layer is judgement work (investigating breaks, triaging alerts, explaining a forecast), and buyers now demand that any AI there show its reasoning. For a 24-hour Agentverse Finance build, the strongest idea is a **"VaR and stress gate"**: Python computes risk, proves itself on held-out market data with standard bank backtests, and blocks a trade on screen until a human signs off. It lines up almost exactly with Jane Street's hint ("anomaly & VaR checks proven in Python") and with every rubric line. A margin-call stress agent comes a close second.

A note on evidence quality before going further: many pain-point surveys below are run or paid for by software vendors (OSTTRA/Acuiti, Eventus, Saifr, SmartStream, Duco, NICE). Their descriptions of the problem are consistent with independent sources, but their "we cut X by 76–85%" results are marketing claims with no shared baseline. The research also failed to collect first-hand complaints from Reddit or Wall Street Oasis, so frontline voices are missing.

## Ten roles decide where the cash goes, and four can sink a firm in days

It helps to sort the roles by **how fast a wrong decision turns into lost cash**.

**Fast (hours to days): cash and collateral.** The *treasury or funding desk* decides how much cash and short-term borrowing the firm holds and where. *Liquidity risk* asks "how much cash could leave in 30 days?" and sets buffers. *Collateral and margin managers* calculate, pay and dispute margin, the cash or bonds you post daily to cover moves on derivatives and loans. *Prime brokers* lend to hedge funds against collateral and decide when to demand more. When these go wrong, it happens fast. Silicon Valley Bank announced a $1.8bn securities loss on 8 March 2023, depositors pulled **more than $40bn the next day**, and it failed on the 10th. Supervisors had already raised six findings on its liquidity stress testing and contingency funding ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/testimony/barr20230328a.htm)). In the 2022 UK pension (LDI) crisis, gilt yields rose more than 100 basis points (1 percentage point) in four days, which is roughly a 20% price fall on a 20-year gilt. Funds sold about £25bn of gilts in five weeks, and that selling caused about half the price fall ([Bank Underground](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2024/07/26/what-caused-the-ldi-crisis/)). The Bank of England puts the margin calls in that episode at roughly **£66bn**, about 80% of it on repo ([Bank of England](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/bank-overground/2025/monitoring-collateral-calls)). The lesson for collateral teams: having enough bonds was not enough. Getting them to the right place in time, through slow trustee approvals, was the real bottleneck.

The most striking case is Archegos. In August 2020 Credit Suisse engineers proposed regularly raising margin on certain swaps, a project estimated at **$150,000**. It was never approved, and seven months later Archegos defaulted and Credit Suisse lost about $5.5bn ([Risk.net, paywalled summary](https://www.risk.net/risk-management/7861786/archegos-report-details-margin-failings-at-credit-suisse)). That is the clearest example in the research of how a cheap control relates to a huge loss.

**Slower (weeks to months), but just as costly: risk, P&L and operations.** *Market risk* staff measure VaR ("value at risk", the loss you should only exceed on about 1 day in 100) and enforce limits. *Product controllers* explain each day's profit and loss independently of the traders. *Operations and reconciliation* staff match the firm's records with counterparties', and *settlements* make sure cash and securities actually change hands. At UBS, a London trader ran up $2.3bn of losses over three months. Operations saw its job as helping trades through, not challenging them; fake internal trades could be booked with no matching other side; and $1.6bn of P&L "suspensions" were accepted without question ([FSA Final Notice](https://mondovisione.com/news/uks-financial-services-authority-fines-ubs-297-million-for-significant-failin-20121126/)). At JPMorgan, errors in a new VaR model masked more than $6bn of losses ([GlobalCapital](https://www.globalcapital.com/article/28mss1vykxr2fjf5mr2v4/derivatives/var-model-errors-masked-derivatives-losses-says-jpmorgan)). The often-repeated story that the model was an Excel sheet that divided by a sum instead of an average was not checked against the primary report, so treat it as unverified. At Citi, a 1997-era loan system sent the whole $900m principal unless staff ticked override boxes, and three people approved it ([PRMIA case study](https://www.prmia.org/common/Uploaded%20files/ORM%20Designation/PRMIA_Citibank-Revlon_021122.pdf)). Across the industry, a Firebrand Research estimate puts the cost of fixing failed equity trades at **over $96bn in 2023** ([Euromoney, paywalled](https://www.euromoney.com/article/2d14p6lxsa4hxjmist4hs/treasury/settlement-failures-more-common-and-more-costly)).

**Compliance and pre-trade controls** check orders against the rules and keep "kill switches" working. Knight Capital's router sent over 4 million orders while trying to fill 212 customer orders. The SEC found that nothing compared outgoing orders with incoming ones and that capital limits could not actually block orders ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)).

**Corporate treasurers** at ordinary companies decide which banks hold the cash and how much. Roku had about **26% of its $1.9bn cash** at SVB, far above the $250,000 deposit insurance limit ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/amp/2023/03/10/roku-says-26percent-cash-reserves-stuck-in-silicon-valley-bank.html)). Since then, 61% of treasury teams rank safety first for short-term cash, and limits on how much sits with one bank are spreading ([AFP via TreasuryCurve](https://www.treasurycurve.com/blog/what-the-2025-afp-liquidity-survey-tells-us-about-treasury-priorities/)).

Read together, the incidents show three repeating failure shapes, and each one is a product idea: **a warning existed but nobody was forced to act on it** (Knight, SVB, Archegos), **two systems disagreed and nobody reconciled them** (UBS), and **a manual step in old software let a huge mistake through** (Citi, the JPMorgan model). One gap: the research found no public incident data from Jane Street, Citadel or Optiver, and no job descriptions from them, so the role descriptions above are general industry knowledge anchored to bank and fund cases.

## Exceptions, blind spots and false alarms dominate the daily complaints

The most consistent daily complaint is **exception handling**, meaning all the trades, payments and margin calls that don't flow through automatically and need a person. An industry standards body, FMSB, found that **about 45% of uncleared margin calls in June 2024 were disputed**, and only 74% of the money called was actually collected on average. Most disputes start in the calculation itself: different FX rate snapshot times (22%), different models (19%) and different price sources (14%) ([FMSB 2025](https://fmsb.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Uncleared-Margin-Spotlight-Review_May-2025_FINAL.pdf)). The same review links over half of settlement failures to bad standing settlement instructions (SSIs, the stored "where to send the money" details). In a vendor-commissioned survey of 45 sell-side firms, 82% still confirm more than a tenth of their bilateral derivative trades by hand, and 66% say ops teams spend over 10% of their time on exceptions ([Acuiti/OSTTRA 2026](https://osttra.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Acuiti-Post-Trade-Process-Report.pdf)). A reconciliation vendor admits that exceptions needing judgement "remain stubbornly resistant" to automation ([Asset Servicing Times](https://www.assetservicingtimes.com/specialistfeatures/specialistfeature.php?specialist_id=710)).

In **corporate treasury**, the complaint is not seeing your own cash. This is the most reliable number in the whole set, because two independent Big Four surveys agree: only **17%** (EY, 1,200+ treasurers) and **22%** (KPMG) have full real-time visibility of cash ([EY 2025](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-gl/campaigns/cfo-agenda/documents/ey-gl-dna-of-the-treasurer-survey-09-2025.pdf); [KPMG via CTMfile](https://ctmfile.com/story/treasury-transformation-gains-pace-but-certain-gaps-persist)). 65% don't produce highly accurate 12-month forecasts. PwC found that 94% own a treasury management system (TMS) yet many still gather forecast data by hand, and 76% blame poor data quality ([PwC 2025](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/services/consulting/finance-accounting-transformation/library/2025-global-treasury-survey.html)). Banks have the same blindness intraday. The largest banks held about $4tn of liquidity buffers, with up to 30% set aside just to settle payments, partly because they cannot see intraday positions in real time ([PostTrade 360](https://posttrade360.com/news/regulation/banks-still-hitting-the-buffers-on-intraday-liquidity-management/)).

In **compliance**, the complaint is false alarms. In a survey run by the surveillance vendor Eventus, 70% of 20 banks and brokers reported false-positive rates above 25% ([The Full FX](https://thefullfx.com/survey-sees-compliance-alert-fatigue/)). Research by another vendor, Saifr, found some firms at **up to 99%** false positives, with analysts spending up to 60% of their time on non-issues. One tier-1 bank raised 7,000 alerts from 5.5 million messages in a single day ([RegTech Analyst](https://regtechanalyst.com/?p=87544)). These numbers measure different things (trade surveillance across firms vs. worst-case chat surveillance), so treat them as a range. **Product control and market-risk staff** are almost certainly heavy spreadsheet users too, but the research found no citable data on their pain. That is a real gap, not evidence that they have none.

## Software handles the routine; judgement work is still done by hand

Existing products split cleanly. The **rule-based layer is mature**: bank connectivity, payments, trade capture and simple matching. Duco's matching platform has 10,000+ users, and Murex's trading platform reports 60,000+ daily users ([IT Brief UK](https://itbrief.co.uk/story/duco-says-agentic-ai-cuts-bank-reconciliation-work-76); [rfp.wiki](https://www.rfp.wiki/investment/capital-markets-software/murex)). The **judgement layer is not**. NeuGroup found that **55% of treasury teams use no automation at all for cash forecasting**, and Excel survives because it is "essentially free" and gives control ([NeuGroup](https://connect.neugroup.com/public/blogs/cash-forecasting-automation-is-not-automatic)). Reconciliation breaks still need "human judgement", and AML screening runs at roughly a 95% false-positive benchmark (this framing comes from a vendor's opinion piece) ([Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202604/under-amla-95-false-positives-become-a-regulators-problem)).

Three critiques of today's tools matter for an AI product. The first is **cost and slow rollout**. Enterprise treasury systems are quoted at $80K–$500K+ a year, with global rollouts taking 9–18 months and often running 30–60% over. These figures come from a firm that sells negotiation services, so they are indicative only ([VendorBenchmark](https://vendorbenchmark.com/vendors/gtreasury-pricing)). This pushes mid-sized companies back to spreadsheets, which is why 2026 startup money (Embat €30M, Bracket $7M) targets that segment ([The Global Treasurer](https://www.theglobaltreasurer.com/2026/05/15/embat-secures-e30m-series-b-to-scale-agentic-ai-across-uk-and-european-treasury/); [Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/bracket-7-million-seed-funding-raised-to-build-ai-native-treasury-infrastructure-for-mid-market-finance-teams)). The second is **black-box AI**. One treasurer said an ML forecast "can get pretty close… but it's not very actionable" because it can't show why ([NeuGroup](https://connect.neugroup.com/public/blogs/cash-forecasting-automation-is-not-automatic)). Under the EU's new AML regime, firms must give reasoning for every dismissed alert that a regulator can follow unaided ([Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202604/under-amla-95-false-positives-become-a-regulators-problem)). The third is **design pattern convergence**. The incumbents' new "agentic" products (Duco, Kyriba, NICE) all put AI agents *on top of* a deterministic rule engine, with humans approving ([IT Brief UK](https://itbrief.co.uk/story/duco-says-agentic-ai-cuts-bank-reconciliation-work-76); [NICE](https://www.nice.com/press-releases/nice-actimize-empowers-surveil-x-with-generative-ai-launching-a-new-era-in-market-abuse-and-conduct-risk-detection)). Their headline gains (76%, 80%, 85%) are unverified and use different baselines.

The clearest open gap is on the **bank and fund side**. The research found almost no funded AI-agent startups for margin and collateral, market risk (VaR) or product control, even though those are where the biggest incident losses sit. That could be a real opening, or a sign that selling to banks is hard and the incumbents own the space. Either way, it is an under-crowded area for a hackathon pitch. This architecture, "maths in code, LLM explains, human approves", is also exactly what the Agentverse rubric rewards.

## Five buildable agents, ranked against the Finance rubric

Ground rules for all five: the **numbers come from Python** (pandas/numpy/scipy), never the LLM. The LLM (Gemini) only reads documents or images, routes the workflow and writes plain-English explanations that quote the Python output. Every action goes to an append-only log. Any synthetic data is flagged `SYNTHETIC=True` in the schema and shown on screen. Scores below are my own judgement on a 1–5 scale per rubric line, weighted to a total out of 100. The weights are: Traceable reasoning 25%, Unseen-data testing 25%, Risk controls 15%, Relevance & impact 20%, Ambition 15%.

### 1. VaR and stress gate: the Jane Street hint, built literally

The **named user** is a market-risk analyst at a trading firm who signs off a trader's request to increase a position before the market opens. The **decision** is approve, cut down or reject a trade that would push the desk's 99% one-day VaR over its limit. The **data** is Binance Vision daily and 1-minute crypto history (free, with sha256 checksums) plus central-bank rates. Watch the trap that spot timestamps switched to microseconds in 2025 ([Binance public data](https://github.com/binance/binance-public-data/)). The **benchmark** is plain historical-simulation VaR on a 250-day window, tested walk-forward on the most recent months the model never saw. The **metrics** are the standard bank backtests. One is the Basel traffic light: over 250 days, 0–4 breaches is green, 5–9 amber, 10+ red ([BIS MAR32](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework/81326/chapter.pdf)). The others are the Kupiec test (are breaches as rare as promised?) and the Christoffersen test (do breaches cluster?) ([MathWorks](https://www.mathworks.com/help/risk/overview-of-var-backtesting.html)), reported with p-values and bootstrap ranges as the stated uncertainty. "Real costs" means applying a declared 5/10/20 bps trading cost to any de-risking trade the agent suggests. A Python anomaly check also flags days when the VaR model's own inputs look broken (gaps, stale prices, unit mix-ups), which is a direct nod to the JPMorgan model failure. The **controls** are a hard VaR limit enforced as an `assert` in code that the LLM cannot override, a kill switch, and a log line for every decision. The **stress layer** replays named shocks: a "100bp-in-four-days" rate shock modelled on September 2022, and the worst historical crypto week. The **Gemini angle** is to accept a trader's screenshot of a position blotter (image), turn it into a structured trade, let Python compute VaR before and after, and have Gemini explain the result only after a Python "math gate" confirms its quoted numbers match the code's. An interactive chart shows the return distribution with the VaR line moving as the trade size slider changes, and the trade waits for a human "approve" click.

**30-second demo:** the trader uploads a screenshot asking to double a position. The gauge jumps from green to red, the trade is **blocked by code**, and Gemini says "this would exceed the $X limit; the Sept-2022-style shock costs $Y". The analyst shrinks the size until it turns green, then signs off, and the log updates live. Next to it sits the backtest panel: "tested on 6 unseen months, 3 breaches, green zone, Kupiec p = 0.4". This idea is the safest bet because public data is excellent and the benchmarks are textbook. Its weakness is ambition, since VaR dashboards are familiar. The stress-test and screenshot-to-trade pieces lift it.

### 2. Margin-call stress and collateral agent: the LDI crisis on one screen

The **named user** is a collateral manager at a pension fund's LDI manager, or at a hedge fund, at 7am. The **decision** is how much cash or gilts to move and where before today's margin calls land, and whether to sell assets or ask the client for more money. The **data** is CME SPAN risk parameter files, which are real and free via DataMine ([CME advisory](https://www.cmegroup.com/notices/clearing/2021/12/Chadv21-471.html)), real price histories, and a synthetic portfolio, because real client portfolios are not public. The call is estimated the way the Bank of England does it: position size multiplied by the percentage change in collateral value ([Bank of England](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/bank-overground/2025/monitoring-collateral-calls)). The **benchmark** is "yesterday's margin carried forward". The **metrics** are error on next-day calls, the missed-call rate (days you were short) and the "over-funding cost" of idle cash, all measured on held-out real price days, with error bands. The **controls** are a minimum cash-buffer floor in code, a cap on forced asset sales per day, and every proposed transfer requiring sign-off. The **Gemini angle** is strong. It reads a counterparty's margin-call notice as a PDF or image, and Python checks the number using the same model. That attacks the documented root causes of the 45% dispute rate (FX snap time, model and price mismatches) ([FMSB](https://fmsb.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Uncleared-Margin-Spotlight-Review_May-2025_FINAL.pdf)). It then flags the gap and drafts a dispute.

**30-second demo:** press "Replay Sept 2022". Yields jump, the cash bar drains toward zero, and the agent shows "Day 3: short £40m, trustee approval takes 2 days, act now". It proposes moving gilts from a pooled fund and refuses a sale that breaches the daily cap. Meanwhile an incoming margin notice is flagged as "£2.1m too high: they used a 4pm FX snap, we used 5pm". It scores high on relevance and ambition. Its unseen-data score suffers because the portfolio is synthetic, which you must say clearly on screen.

### 3. AML alert triage: the best benchmark, the most crowded idea

The **named user** is an AML analyst at a bank with a fixed daily review capacity. The **decision** is which alerts to escalate and which to close with a written reason. The **data** is IBM's synthetic IT-AML dataset (HI-Small: 5M transactions, 1 in 981 laundering), using its official time-ordered 60/20/20 split ([arXiv 2306.16424](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.16424v1)). The **benchmark** is the published minority-class F1 baselines (MLP 24.1, GIN 39.3, PNA 51.9) plus simple fan-in/fan-out rules. The **metrics** are precision@k, where k is one analyst's daily capacity, and AUC-PR, averaged over several seeds. The **controls** are a cap on daily auto-closures and a rule that high-value or certain pattern types can never be auto-closed. A good fit is that every dismissal carries a regulator-readable reason, which is exactly what the AMLA regime demands ([Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202604/under-amla-95-false-positives-become-a-regulators-problem)). There is also a possible "everyday inclusion" story: fewer false alarms means fewer ordinary customers frozen out. That is a reasonable argument, but the research has no data to back it.

**30-second demo:** a queue of 200 alerts shrinks to the top 20, each with the money-flow pattern drawn as a graph. Clicking one shows "fan-in, 14 accounts, 3 hours" next to the Python feature values. The weaknesses are that graph models are hard to make traceable, it is the most common hackathon finance project, and it fits Jane Street's trading focus less well.

### 4. Payment pre-flight checker: the most dramatic demo, the weakest data

The **named user** is a loan-operations payments approver like Citi's "checker". The **decision** is whether to release an outgoing payment. Python compares each payment to what the loan schedule says is due (interest vs. principal), to the counterparty's history, and to recent changes in settlement instructions. The **data** is PaySim (synthetic mobile-money transactions; its built-in "transfer > 200,000" flag is a ready-made rules baseline) plus a synthetic loan book with injected errors ([PaySim README](https://github.com/BBQtime/Synthetic-Financial-Datasets-For-Fraud-Detection)). The **metrics** are precision and recall per error type, plus false holds per day. The **controls** are the strongest of any idea: a hard hold on any payment above X times the expected amount, a second approver for overrides, and a full log. The **Gemini angle** is that it reads the payment screen as an image (the exact UI that misled Citi's staff) and the instruction PDF, then Python checks the amounts.

**30-second demo:** a reviewer clicks approve on a "$7.8m interest payment" and the agent blocks it with "this screen will send **$900m**, the full principal; expected $7.8m". It is relevant and memorable. Its weakness is that everything is synthetic, so it scores low on unseen-data testing.

### 5. Small-business cash concentration guard: the inclusion play

The **named user** is a startup finance lead who cannot afford an $80K+ treasury system. The **decision** is how much cash to keep at each bank and when to move it. The **data** is real SEC EDGAR filings for cash and operating cash flow (10 requests per second, declared User-Agent required) ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/os/accessing-edgar-data)). The **benchmark** is a naive or same-quarter-last-year forecast, judged by forecast error and whether actual values fall inside the stated range on the held-out latest year. The **controls** are a per-bank limit in code (the Roku/SVB lesson) and every move logged. It fits "everyday inclusion" best, but quarterly data gives few test points and the idea is the least ambitious.

## Conclusion

The research changes where to aim. The instinct for a "finance AI" hackathon is to build a trading bot. The documented money, though, is lost at the *gates*: the moment a margin increase, a VaR breach, a P&L oddity or a payment needs someone to say no. Those gates failed because warnings were not forced into decisions and numbers were not cross-checked. A gate agent therefore has a sharp story ("this is the $150,000 fix Credit Suisse never approved"), and its architecture of deterministic maths, LLM explanation and human sign-off matches what incumbents are now shipping and what regulators are starting to require. The rubric rewards the same thing.

Practically, build Idea 1 as the core and use Idea 2's stress replay as the "wow" moment if time allows. Both share the same Python risk engine, data download and approval log, so four people can split engine, backtests, Gemini document/image intake and the interactive front end. Download the Binance history and one CME SPAN file before the clock starts. Spend the final hours on the honesty slide: which data is real, which is synthetic, the test period, the p-values and what the model cannot do. On a rubric that gives half its weight to traceability and unseen-data testing, that slide is worth more than an extra feature.
